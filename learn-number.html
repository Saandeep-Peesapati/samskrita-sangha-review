What you’ll learn

  • Distinguish singular, dual, and plural.
  • Recognise number in basic subject forms.
  • Match a third-person verb to its subject’s number.

Helpful first: Words & simple sentences. These are suggestions; you can explore in any order.

Every example pairs Devanagari with IAST and an English meaning. Word boundaries stay visible; full external sandhi is postponed so you can focus on forms.

01

Exactly two has its own form

Sanskrit distinguishes one, exactly two, and more than two. The names are singular, dual, and plural. English usually groups two and three together as plural; Sanskrit does not. When you mean exactly two boys, you normally choose the dual noun and a dual verb.

Three numbers in subject forms
ModelOneExactly twoThree or more
Boyबालः bālaḥबालौ bālauबालाः bālāḥ
Girlबालिका bālikāबालिके bālikeबालिकाः bālikāḥ
Fruitफलम् phalamफले phaleफलानि phalāni

These are nominative forms: forms suitable for the subject. You do not obtain every plural by adding a single universal suffix to the singular. Each stem type has a pattern, and different cases can use different endings. The noun branches later in the tree develop these models.

02

The subject and verb work as a pair

A third-person subject is someone or something other than the speaker “I” or the listener “you”. In our present-tense reading model, use paṭhati for one, paṭhataḥ for two, and paṭhanti for more than two. The noun ending and verb ending together tell you the number.

Read each sentence aloud and point first to the noun ending, then to the verb ending. The final ḥ in paṭhataḥ belongs to the verb form; it does not turn the verb into a noun. Endings must be understood as part of a pattern, rather than assigned a single meaning everywhere.

03

Count the subject, not the nearby object

The verb agrees with its subject, even when the object has another number. One girl may read several books. Several girls may read one book. The number of books does not control the verb because the books are not the subject of these active sentences.

This is a useful way to diagnose mistakes. If a sentence has a plural object, do not immediately choose a plural verb. First locate the subject and count it. Then return to the object and give it its own appropriate form.

04

Practise one change at a time

Start with bālaḥ paṭhati. Change the meaning to “two boys read”; change both words, not just the noun. Next make it “the boys read”. Finally use the feminine noun forms from the table with the same three verbs. Since the verb forms here do not change for gender, the number pattern stays familiar.

You do not have to add a numeral meaning “two” whenever you use a dual. The dual ending already signals two. Numerals have their own grammar, which lies outside this small foundation course. Today’s goal is to make one/two/many an automatic question whenever you choose a noun or verb form.

Common mistakes

  • Dual means exactly two; plural normally means more than two.
  • Changing the subject’s number usually also requires changing the verb.
  • A plural object does not by itself require a plural verb.

Which number is used for exactly two?
  • Singular
  • Dual
  • Plural

Answer: Dual The dual is the ordinary form for exactly two.

Complete बालौ … — bālau … (“The two boys read”).
  • पठति — paṭhati
  • पठन्ति — paṭhanti
  • पठतः — paṭhataḥ

Answer: पठतः — paṭhataḥ A dual subject takes the third-person dual form paṭhataḥ.

In “The girl reads books”, what determines the verb’s number?
  • The one girl
  • The many books
  • The number of words

Answer: The one girl The subject, the girl, is singular.

Make it your own

Practise on your own

Write an answer or say it aloud before opening the solution.

1. Change बालिका पठति। — bālikā paṭhati to “The two girls read”.

Reveal solution 1
बालिके पठतः। bālike paṭhataḥ. — The two girls read. Both subject and verb become dual.

2. Translate बालाः पुस्तकं पठन्ति। — bālāḥ pustakaṃ paṭhanti.

Reveal solution 2
The boys read a book. The subject is plural; the direct object is singular.
