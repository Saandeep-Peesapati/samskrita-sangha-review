What you’ll learn

  • Plan a sentence from its meaning.
  • Choose case and number for each noun.
  • Check agreement and explain your choices.

Helpful first: Masculine nouns in a, Neuter nouns in a, Feminine nouns in ā, I, you, he, she & it, Present-tense verbs. These are suggestions; you can explore in any order.

Every example pairs Devanagari with IAST and an English meaning. Word boundaries stay visible; full external sandhi is postponed so you can focus on forms.

01

Start with a thought you can explain

You now have enough grammar to create small, complete sentences. Begin with a clear meaning, such as “The girl reads a book”. Resist translating an English sentence word by word from left to right. Instead, identify the action and its participants, then choose each Sanskrit form for its job.

Use this sequence: choose the verb’s meaning; identify the subject; identify any direct object; choose number and case for the nouns; choose person and number for the verb. Add possession or location only after that core is working. Finally read the whole sentence and translate it back into English.

Keep in mind

These exercises continue to keep word boundaries and grammatical forms visible. Full external sandhi is outside this course. Some combinations therefore appear in a deliberately separated teaching form rather than as continuous classical prose.

02

Work through one sentence

Plan: “The two girls read books”
DecisionReasonChosen form
SubjectGirls: feminine, exactly two, nominativeबालिके bālike
ObjectBooks: neuter, plural, accusativeपुस्तकानि pustakāni
VerbRead: third person, dual, presentपठतः paṭhataḥ

The noun bālike looks the same in nominative and accusative dual, but here it is the subject because the girls are reading. Pustakāni is the direct object. This is why understanding the meaning and verb is essential even when you recognise all the endings.

Change only the subject to one girl: bālikā pustakāni paṭhati. Both the noun and verb change to singular; the object stays plural. Change only the object to one book: bālike pustakaṃ paṭhataḥ. Now the dual subject and verb remain unchanged.

03

Add possession or location

Each addition has its own job. The genitive possessor does not become the subject merely because it names a person. The locative does not become the direct object merely because it occurs before the verb. If a longer sentence becomes confusing, temporarily remove the extra information and find the core subject and verb again.

A noun and its adjective agree with each other, while the subject and the finite verb agree in person and number. These are related checks but not identical ones. In sundarā bālikā paṭhati, sundarā matches the feminine singular subject; paṭhati remains third-person singular.

04

Learn from a deliberate mistake

Consider बालौ पठति। bālau paṭhati.. The noun means “two boys” as subject, but the verb means “he/she/it reads”. The number labels disagree. Repair the verb to produce बालौ पठतः। bālau paṭhataḥ. — The two boys read.. A useful correction explains the reason rather than replacing a word by guesswork.

Another common error is to use a masculine plural subject ending with a neuter noun. Books are pustakāni in the nominative and accusative plural, not pustakāḥ. When uncertain, return to the noun’s stem and gender, then pick the correct model.

Word order can vary in Sanskrit. Our subject–object–verb order is a practice aid rather than a rule that every text must follow. As your reading grows, rely more on case endings, agreement, the verb’s meaning, and context.

05

Your first independent paragraph

Write three connected thoughts: “I read a book. The girl writes. The two boys play.” Choose each form before looking at the solution in the practice section. Then swap one subject or object and explain every ending that must change.

This foundation is a beginning. You can now approach a simple sentence by identifying its action, participants, and relationships. When you meet an unfamiliar stem or tense, consult a reference rather than forcing it into the patterns here. Revisit any branch where choosing forms still feels uncertain; completing the tree never locks a lesson away.

Common mistakes

  • Check the subject’s number, not the nearest noun’s number.
  • Use the correct gender-and-stem pattern for each noun.
  • Do not treat our teaching word order as mandatory in all Sanskrit.
Pause & check

Check your understanding

Choose one answer for each question. You can retry as often as you like.

1. Choose “The two girls read books”.

2. What must change when अहं पठामि (“I read”) becomes “You read”?

3. Which form adds “in the village”?

With JavaScript off, try each question, then open its answer.

Choose “The two girls read books”.
  • बालिके पुस्तकानि पठतः। — bālike pustakāni paṭhataḥ.
  • बालिके पुस्तकानि पठति। — bālike pustakāni paṭhati.
  • बालिका पुस्तकानि पठतः। — bālikā pustakāni paṭhataḥ.

Answer: बालिके पुस्तकानि पठतः। — bālike pustakāni paṭhataḥ. Both bālike and paṭhataḥ are dual; pustakāni is the plural object.

What must change when अहं पठामि (“I read”) becomes “You read”?
  • Only the noun’s gender
  • The subject pronoun and the verb’s person
  • Only the spelling of the vowel

Answer: The subject pronoun and the verb’s person Use tvaṃ paṭhasi: second-person singular pronoun and verb.

Which form adds “in the village”?
  • ग्रामस्य — grāmasya
  • ग्रामम् — grāmam
  • ग्रामे — grāme

Answer: ग्रामे — grāme Grāme is locative singular and supplies the location.

Make it your own

Practise on your own

Write an answer or say it aloud before opening the solution.

1. Translate: “I read a book. The girl writes. The two boys play.”

Reveal solution 1
अहं पुस्तकं पठामि। ahaṃ pustakaṃ paṭhāmi. — I read a book.
बालिका लिखति। bālikā likhati. — The girl writes.
बालौ क्रीडतः। bālau krīḍataḥ. — The two boys play. The verbs are first-person singular, third-person singular, and third-person dual.

2. Write “The girls read my book in the village”.

Reveal solution 2
बालिकाः ग्रामे मम पुस्तकं पठन्ति। bālikāḥ grāme mama pustakaṃ paṭhanti. — The girls read my book in the village. Bālikāḥ is nominative plural, grāme locative singular, mama genitive, pustakaṃ accusative singular, and paṭhanti third-person plural.

3. Correct बालौ पठति। — bālau paṭhati and explain the problem.

Reveal solution 3
बालौ पठतः। bālau paṭhataḥ. — The two boys read. The dual subject needs a dual verb, not singular paṭhati.
