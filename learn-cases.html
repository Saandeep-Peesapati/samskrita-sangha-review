01

A case connects a noun to the sentence

A case is a grammatical form that helps express how a noun relates to other words. In English, a preposition such as “with”, “from”, or “in” often does this work. Sanskrit frequently uses an ending instead. A noun’s form also reflects number and its declension pattern, so case is one part of a larger decision.

Begin with two questions you already know: who acts, and what is directly affected? In our simple active sentences, the subject is nominative and the direct object is accusative. Other cases let us add an instrument, a recipient, a starting point, a possessor, or a location.

Keep in mind

We teach seven numbered cases plus the vocative, a form used for addressing someone. “Eight cases” is convenient beginner shorthand; the traditional system treats the vocative as address associated with the first case.

02

Eight useful questions

Core meanings, using the masculine stem bāla (boy)
CaseUseful questionOne boy: form and typical sense
1 · NominativeWho acts?बालः bālaḥ — the boy, as subject
2 · AccusativeWhom / what directly?बालम् bālam — the boy, as object
3 · InstrumentalBy / with what?बालेन bālena — by or with the boy
4 · DativeTo / for whom?बालाय bālāya — to or for the boy
5 · AblativeFrom whom / what?बालात् bālāt — from the boy
6 · GenitiveWhose?बालस्य bālasya — of the boy
7 · LocativeIn / on / at what?बाले bāle — in or on the boy
VocativeWhom am I addressing?बाल bāla — O boy!

The questions are starting points, not word-for-word translation laws. A verb or a construction can require a case for reasons that an English preposition does not predict. For example, an accusative can also indicate a destination with some motion verbs. Learn the central meanings now and check unfamiliar constructions later.

03

See cases in small, understandable pieces

Notice that these forms cannot be built by attaching the same letters to every noun. Bālena and lekhanyā both express an instrumental role but belong to different stem patterns. This lesson explains the jobs; the next three lessons explain common sets of forms.

04

One form can have more than one job

A form does not always identify its case uniquely. Neuter nouns have the same nominative and accusative forms in each number. Some feminine singular forms also share endings across cases. Context, the verb, and the other words help you decide which role is intended.

Use a four-step routine: identify the meaning, identify the role, decide the number, and choose the form from the correct noun pattern. For “the boy’s book”, the boy is the possessor: choose genitive singular bālasya. The book has its own role in the larger sentence; the possessor does not determine the book’s case.

Spend a minute giving an English phrase for each case before memorising endings. Being able to say why a word needs a genitive or a locative is more useful than reciting a table without understanding it.

Common mistakes

  • Do not translate every English “to” automatically with the dative.
  • Case and number are separate decisions.
  • A repeated form may express different cases; use context.
Pause & check

Check your understanding

Choose one answer for each question. You can retry as often as you like.

1. Which case expresses “of the boy” in बालस्य — bālasya?

2. Which case normally marks the direct object of “reads”?

3. Which role does ग्रामे — grāme have in “The girl lives in the village”?

With JavaScript off, try each question, then open its answer.

Which case expresses “of the boy” in बालस्य — bālasya?
  • Genitive
  • Accusative
  • Vocative

Answer: Genitive The genitive commonly expresses possession or a relationship: “of the boy”.

Which case normally marks the direct object of “reads”?
  • Locative
  • Accusative
  • Ablative

Answer: Accusative What is read is the direct object, normally in the accusative.

Which role does ग्रामे — grāme have in “The girl lives in the village”?
  • The person addressed
  • The direct object
  • The location

Answer: The location Grāme is locative singular: “in the village”.

Make it your own

Practise on your own

Write an answer or say it aloud before opening the solution.

1. Choose cases for “the girl”, “a book”, and “in the village” in “The girl reads a book in the village”.

Reveal solution 1
The girl: nominative. A book: accusative. In the village: locative. The subject, object, and location have different roles.

2. Explain why बालस्य पुस्तकम् — bālasya pustakam means “the boy’s book”.

Reveal solution 2
बालस्य bālasya is genitive singular, marking the possessor. पुस्तकम् pustakam is “book”; the phrase alone does not tell whether the book is a subject or object in a larger sentence.
